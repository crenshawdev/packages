## THE COPYRIGHT REBUTTAL — verified, citation-backed (deep-research, 75/75 claims survived
## adversarial verification at high confidence, 0 refutations)

The COSMIC lead made two claims. ONE has a true kernel; BOTH are overstated into falsehood.
John's voice move: concede the true kernel, then demolish the overreach. The overreach lives in
six words — "all," "public domain," and "forfeit all copyright."

CLAIM A — "all code generated by a LLM is public domain and cannot be copyrighted."
TRUE KERNEL (concede this): in the US, *purely* AI-generated material that lacks human authorship
is not protected by copyright. The US Copyright Office requires human authorship; a machine cannot
be an "author."
WHY THE FULL CLAIM IS FALSE:
- The rule is narrower than "all." AI-*assisted* code IS copyrightable where a human exercises
  creative control — selecting, arranging, or modifying the output, or contributing their own
  expression. Copyrightability turns on the degree of human creative control, case by case — not a
  categorical bar. [USCO, "Copyright Registration Guidance for Works Containing AI-Generated
  Materials," 88 FR 16190, Mar 16 2023; USCO "Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2:
  Copyrightability," Jan 29 2025.] Sources: copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf ;
  federalregister.gov/documents/2023/03/16/2023-05321/
- Source code is a "literary work" copyrightable under 17 U.S.C. §102(a). [law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/102]
- The controlling case AGREES with the narrow rule and REFUTES the broad one: Thaler v.
  Perlmutter (D.C. Cir., Mar 2025, No. 23-5233) affirmed that AI *alone* can't be the author, but
  EXPRESSLY limited its holding to works authored *exclusively* by AI and stated the human-
  authorship requirement "does not prohibit copyrighting works made by or with the assistance of"
  AI, so long as a human created/operated/used it. [media.cadc.uscourts.gov/opinions/docs/2025/03/23-5233.pdf]
- "Public domain" is not even globally true. Multiple jurisdictions grant copyright to
  computer-generated works: UK CDPA 1988 §9(3) (author = the person who made the arrangements
  necessary for creation) [legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/9]; New Zealand Copyright Act
  1994 s5(2)(a); Hong Kong Copyright Ordinance (Cap 528) s198; and India registered the AI-
  assisted work SURYAST. So the flat "it's public domain" claim fails outside the US too.

CLAIM B — "It is legally required to disclose which lines were generated else you will legally
forfeit all copyright of all code in your project."
THIS ONE HAS NO TRUE KERNEL. It conflates registration formalities with the existence of copyright.
- Copyright SUBSISTS AUTOMATICALLY upon fixation. 17 U.S.C. §102(a): protection exists the moment
  an original work is fixed in a tangible medium. No registration, no disclosure, is a precondition
  for copyright to EXIST. [law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/102] Thaler reaffirmed the author gains
  exclusive rights immediately upon creation (citing Fourth Estate; 17 U.S.C. §201(a)).
- The disclose/disclaim duty is a REGISTRATION formality before the Copyright Office — identify
  human-authored material, disclaim more-than-de-minimis AI material in the *application*. It is
  not a condition of the copyright's existence. [88 FR 16190; Part 2 report.]
- Non-disclosure does NOT destroy the underlying copyright, and CANNOT forfeit "all copyright of
  all code." The actual consequence is loss of REGISTRATION benefits (and, if the inaccuracy was
  *knowingly* included, a 17 U.S.C. §411(b) referral) — not destruction of the copyright. Proof:
  in *Zarya of the Dawn* the Office canceled the original registration but issued a NEW one
  covering the human-authored material, which remained protected.
- Mixing AI-assisted code into a larger human-authored codebase does NOT void copyright in the
  human-authored portions. The human expression remains protected independently of the AI material.

BONUS — the DCO doesn't say what the checklist implies. The Developer Certificate of Origin
(Linux Foundation, v1.1, 2004) certifies PROVENANCE and the RIGHT TO SUBMIT under the project's
license. It contains NO AI-disclosure requirement, NO AI-authorship attestation, and NO copyright-
forfeiture clause. [developercertificate.org] Bundling "I have read the DCO and certify…" next to
an AI-disclosure checkbox conflates two unrelated things; the DCO is silent on AI.

HONEST CAVEATS (per voice guide — flag what's genuinely unsettled, don't overstate the win):
- UK §9(3) is itself contested: a 2024 UK government review floated repealing it; scholars call it
  "more symbolic than functional." Lean on it as "the global-public-domain claim is false," not as
  a robust grant. [authorsalliance.org/2025/05/19/...; KCL "Time to repeal section 9(3)".]
- US AI-copyrightability is an active policy area and registrability is case-by-case — true in BOTH
  directions, which is exactly why a flat "it's all public domain" is wrong.

NET FOR THE POST: the maintainer was directionally right about *purely* AI-generated output in the
US and turned it into a sweeping legal threat that the statute, the controlling case, the Copyright
Office's own guidance, and four other countries' laws all contradict. That's the rebuttal: not
"he's wrong, trust me" — "here's §102, here's Thaler, here's the Copyright Office, here's the UK
statute." Primary sources only; everything above survived 3-vote adversarial verification.
